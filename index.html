About

Why Islamic philanthropy

I entered this field because generosity in Islam is not merely about giving. It is about building systems that let people help others with dignity, trust, and lasting effect. My route in was humanitarian and disaster response, where I saw that communities need more than emergency assistance — they need stronger institutions, better resource mobilisation, and a genuine path to resilience. Islamic philanthropy is where faith, humanity, and social impact meet.

I hold a view that not everyone in this sector shares: philanthropy should not be treated as a fundraising activity. It is a professional ecosystem of trust, resources, people, technology, and impact. An institution's success is not measured by how much money it raises, but by how effectively it converts generosity into sustainable change. Islamic philanthropy also needs more courage to embrace innovation, data, and professional management — without surrendering its ethical and spiritual foundation.

My work today centres on strengthening philanthropic and humanitarian organisations through resource mobilisation, digital fundraising, institutional development, volunteer networks, and community empowerment. What interests me most is the join between traditional philanthropic values and modern systems. What I am working toward is an Islamic philanthropy ecosystem that donors trust, that means something to the people it serves, that draws younger generations in, and that can create impact at scale.

I want to be remembered as someone who turned generosity into impact — by helping people, institutions, and communities move from giving to lasting change. Personal statement

Egypt–Palestine Humanitarian Mission

The context

I joined this mission under the Konsorsium Kebaikan Indonesia untuk Palestina, responding to Gazans affected by the conflict and to Palestinian refugees sheltering in Egypt. The context differed from ordinary humanitarian work. We were not only meeting material need; we were meeting people carrying loss, trauma, displacement, and an unresolved future.

The challenge was keeping assistance both relevant and dignified, while making sure that attention to Gaza did not end when the news cycle moved on. Humanitarian action has to be sustained across borders and across faiths, so that the world keeps seeing that there are human beings here who need solidarity.

What I did

We met and spoke directly with Gazan refugees in Egypt, and carried out visits and coordination near the border. The interventions covered food assistance for affected families; psychosocial programming for children, giving them room to play and be supported after what they had lived through; Qur'an programming for hafiz students, as both spiritual support and educational continuity; and a visit to the Palestinian hospital in Egypt, where many patients were casualties evacuated from Gaza. Alongside this we mobilised and dispatched 5,067 food packages in six container trucks from Egypt into Gaza.

The experience showed me that humanitarian response is not only distribution. It is also presence, listening, keeping hope alive, building networks, and making sure the voices of those affected do not disappear from public attention.

What did not go to plan

Cross-border humanitarian work rarely follows its original design. Access, coordination, security conditions, regulation, timing, and shifting realities on the ground all affected how the programme and the distribution actually ran.

I also came to see that good intentions are not the same as effective intervention. There are moments when we arrive carrying assumptions about what a community needs, while the reality on the ground calls for a different approach. It is a reminder that in humanitarian action we must listen more than we assume.

What I would change

Three things. First, stronger needs assessment and contextual analysis before intervening, so that every programme rests on real needs and on the community's own voice. Second, stronger monitoring and impact measurement — not only recording how much aid was delivered, but understanding what changed for the people who received it. Third, a more integrated strategy connecting relief, psychosocial support, spiritual support, advocacy, and long-term humanitarian engagement.

I do not want a mission to end when the team flies home. The real challenge is sustaining attention, solidarity, and public support for as long as the crisis lasts.

Presence is a form of aid. Witness is a form of responsibility. Solidarity is the energy that keeps a humanitarian movement alive.

The problem

Indonesian zakat institutions, waqf boards, and mosque committees were being told to go digital without being given the professional foundations to do it. Training in the sector tended to stop at the seminar — a day of inspiration, no follow-through, no change in how the institution actually ran.

What I built

I founded the Academy to close that gap: capacity-building programmes covering professional governance, programme utilisation, sustainability, and fundraising management, delivered through webinars, seminars, bootcamps, and in-house training — then continued as institutional consultancy and long-term mentoring rather than ending at the certificate.

Outcome

More than a thousand cumulative participants have come through the programmes, including mosque administrators and philanthropy practitioners across Indonesia. Consultancy engagements have followed with Satu Amal Indonesia, Rumah Quran Rabbani, and Rabithah Peduli, where the work extended into organisational structure, standard operating procedures, core team formation, and donor network strategy.

The problem

A national network is only as strong as its weakest branch. Retail fundraising sat well below its potential, and branches were working against targets without a shared set of channels or a common way of engaging donors.

What I did

I led the network against fundraising and programme impact targets, and developed seven distinct philanthropy channels so branches had more than one route to the donor.

Outcome

Branch retail fundraising rose to 40 per cent of the national total, and retail donor engagement increased by 65 per cent.

The problem

Volunteer movements grow fast and organise slowly. Numbers alone do not make a network deployable — without structure, a large volunteer base cannot be matched to the work that actually needs doing.

What I built

Alongside growing the network across 38 provinces and 390 districts and cities, I established a volunteer clustering system organised by programme area and by profession, so that skills could be matched to need rather than counted in aggregate.

Outcome

A national volunteer society of 81,499 members with a structure that made specialised deployment possible. In parallel, as CEO of Relawan Filantropi Indonesia, I developed a community-based digital philanthropy agent platform integrating online and offline volunteers through qurban agent, affiliate crowdfunding, and digital advocate systems.
